HEADLINE: White House opens 90-day tariff-free window for ground beef imports; ranchers and farm-state Republicans push back

LEDE: President Donald Trump said Friday that the United States will allow up to 300,000 metric tons of product for ground beef to enter over the next 90 days without an out-of-quota tariff, a short-term import opening he tied to high grocery prices. Cattle producers and Republican lawmakers from farm states said the same step would undercut domestic ranchers trying to rebuild herds.

ARTICLE:
WASHINGTON — President Donald Trump announced Friday that his administration will temporarily waive the extra tariff that normally kicks in once beef imports pass a set quota, allowing as much as 300,000 metric tons of ground-beef product into the country over 90 days. In a Truth Social post, he said the government had “a commitment that this beef will be sold at 25 percent below current market prices.”

A White House official, speaking on condition of anonymity because the plan is not yet finalized, told news organizations the product is lean beef trimmings used in ground beef, that foreign exporters had agreed to a 25 percent discount “to be passed along to American consumers,” and that Trump intends to sign an executive order within about two weeks. The official did not name the countries or companies. Trump, asked by reporters Friday, said “there are a few” suppliers and that they would send the “highest quality beef.”

The announcement lands on a tight cattle market. Beef prices have reached record highs as the U.S. herd has shrunk, demand has held up, and cattle movements from Mexico have been limited because of a flesh-eating screwworm outbreak. The American Farm Bureau Federation has said the U.S. cattle inventory is the smallest since 1951. The Bureau of Labor Statistics, cited by ABC News, reported beef and veal prices up 9.4 percent in the latest month versus a year earlier. The United States also maintains a 50 percent tariff on Brazil, a major beef exporter. In February, Trump signed a proclamation that temporarily expanded the tariff-rate quota for lean beef trimmings.

Trump has framed the 90-day window as both consumer relief and breathing room for domestic herds. “This deal will reduce prices for Americans while giving space for our Great American Beef Herd to grow again,” he wrote. At Joint Base Andrews, he said, “We want to get the beef prices down, so we’ll get them down a little bit, and that’s what people want. That’s what the voters want, and that’s what I want.” He added that ranchers “admit that we need a little help.” A White House official described the step as short-term, saying the administration is “simultaneously working with ranchers to expand domestic beef production.” USDA said the quota change would “help address the affordability of beef.” November midterms are a backdrop; grocery prices remain a campaign issue, and the 90-day period would run past Election Day.

Farm-state Republicans and cattle groups rejected that trade-off. Sen. Deb Fischer, R-Neb., said she was “extremely disappointed” and that “flooding the market with foreign beef hurts our livestock industry and undermines the long-term solution: growing the U.S. cattle herd to meet demand.” Sen. Pete Ricketts, R-Neb., who is up for reelection this fall, said short-term policy shifts “do not equal long term solutions” and that “lower quality beef compromises Nebraska farmers and ranchers.” Sen. Tim Sheehy, R-Mont., said the president’s “heart is in the right place” after the Mexican screwworm hit prices, but that imports would “make it more difficult for American ranchers to rebuild our herd” and “harm our ranching families.” Sen. Chuck Grassley, R-Iowa, said U.S. cattlemen “shld always be put 1st.” Rep. Ashley Hinson, R-Iowa, a Trump-endorsed Senate candidate, called the idea “bad” and urged cutting red tape and production costs instead. Senate Majority Leader John Thune, R-S.D., opposed a similar import idea last year, noting his state has “four times as many cattle as people.”

Industry groups lined up with the critics. Justin Tupper, president of the U.S. Cattlemen’s Association, said, “You don’t put America first by putting U.S. cattle producers last.” Colin Woodall, CEO of the National Cattlemen’s Beef Association, said producers share the goal of affordable groceries but that “flooding the market with government-subsidized, below-market beef is not the way to rebuild the American cattle herd,” and that the announcement “sacrifices long-term stability for short term messaging.” Ethan Lane, NCBA’s top lobbyist, told ABC News he had not spoken to anyone who thought the plan would help supply, and that cattle markets moved lower after the post. Bill Bullard, CEO of R-CALF USA, said imports have already contributed to the inventory decline and that more imports “will prevent herd expansion.”

Independent analysts were more cautious about the scale. Glynn Tonsor, a Kansas State University economist who studies cattle and beef, told the Associated Press the 300,000 metric tons is roughly 3 percent of annual U.S. consumption and that his immediate view was the price effect would not be large. David Anderson of Texas A&M questioned whether other countries could redirect that much beef in 90 days. Those assessments are opinions about likely market effects, not confirmed outcomes.

What is not yet public: which exporters signed the 25 percent discount, whether the volume can actually arrive in the window, how USDA will inspect and label the product, and the final text of the executive order. Trump first floated easier beef imports last year and ran into the same farm-state Republican resistance.
