What happened

NASA’s Scientific Visualization Studio posted Friday’s XRISM science package: the Japan-led X-ray Imaging and Spectroscopy Mission has directly observed a giant star’s stellar wind being captured by its compact companion and powering strong X-ray flares — a wind-plasma signature astronomers say has never been seen so clearly. The system is BP Crucis, about 13,000 light-years away in Crux. The primary, Wray 977, is a blue hypergiant roughly 40 times the Sun’s mass and 60 times its size; its companion is the neutron-star pulsar GX 301-2, packing more than a solar mass into a city-sized ball that rotates every 11 minutes. That is the Science filing: a wind, a gulp, and an X-ray flare you can almost storyboard.

Twice each 41.5-day orbit, near closest and farthest approach, the pulsar lights up for several days as it crosses a dense plasma stream sculpted by its gravity. XRISM’s Resolve instrument, jointly developed by NASA and JAXA, watched for about 16 hours on Feb. 1, 2025, near the end of a stronger flare. Highly ionized iron absorption lines are redshifted — gas racing toward the pulsar at around 335,000 mph (540,000 kph). Artist concepts show a messy accretion disk forming, breaking up into direct infall, then rebuilding spinning the other way as the pulsar exits the stream. Why it matters

Binary X-ray machines are usually inferred from brightness curves; Resolve’s iron-line redshift turns “the wind fell in” into a measured velocity arrow.
