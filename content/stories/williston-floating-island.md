What happened

BBC News reports a forested patch on Williston Reservoir in British Columbia that looks like a tiny island and behaves like a raft. Locals spotted it in early August. Satellite images later showed it gone from that first pin. On Monday it turned up about 30 kilometers away, near Ospika. Measurements in the coverage put the mat at roughly 140 meters by 70 meters, trees and all. It is unexplained in the pop-history sense: a floating forest that clocks out of one grid square and checks into another. It is not a navy, not a portal, and not a story about anyone’s government. The source URL for this package is the BBC piece.

BC Hydro spokesperson Bob Gammer, quoted in the same reporting, describes a raft of driftwood and peat, the kind of mat that can form when reservoir water stays high. Williston has seen high water since 2012, he said. The advice is practical: do not climb on it. Peat and driftwood can come apart. The Globe, the Guardian, and CKPG ran parallel versions of the same raft. This desk is filing the BBC URL as the source of record, with the Hydro explanation in the last panel so the unexplained beat stays entertainment, not a lecture and not a coded fight. A reservoir this large can hide a 140-meter mat behind a headland and then reveal it on a new shore. That is still a fun satellite ghost.

The strip is the cute island, the X on the map, the 30 km hop, and a clipboard. Color on the water, the pines, the ranger hat. White gutters. Cartoon trees, not a documentary still. A wandering forest on a Canadian reservoir is the right shape: weird, photographed, given a mundane peat-and-driftwood note, still visually uncanny.
