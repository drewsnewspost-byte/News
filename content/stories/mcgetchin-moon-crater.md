What happened

NASA Science’s Sept. 16 feature is the Science filing: Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter image-processing specialist Robert Wagner spotted an unusually large bright debris halo while comparing global Moon maps, then confirmed the largest newly formed crater yet found in the solar system. Officially named McGetchin after lunar scientist Tom McGetchin, the crater formed on the Moon’s eastern edge sometime between April 11 and May 22, 2024, after a comet or asteroid roughly the size of a three- to six-story building hit the surface.

The crash left a crater about 728 feet wide—two football fields end to end—and about 141 feet deep, deep enough to stack three yellow school buses. Scientists estimate an impact of this magnitude happens on the Moon about once a century or even longer. Follow-up Diviner thermal data found a roughly 4-mile-wide “cold spot” around the crater that runs about 16 degrees Fahrenheit cooler at night because the impact fluffed the regolith. Bright lunar gray, orange debris rays, and blue cold-spot tones. White gutters. The NASA Science URL is the receipt.

The discovery was reported in a pair of Science Advances papers around Sept. 15–16, 2026. LRO has flagged at least 1,000 new impact craters across more than 17 years, but McGetchin stood out across hundreds of pixels in the change maps. Why it matters

A fresh lunar crater with before-and-after orbital proof is the strip scientists rarely get: a pristine impact laboratory still sitting on the surface before other processes erase the details. Conclusion

NASA’s Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter team identified McGetchin crater—the largest newly formed crater found in the solar system—as a roughly 728-foot-wide, 141-foot-deep once-in-a-century impact on the Moon’s eastern edge between April 11 and May 22, 2024, with Diviner data showing a 4-mile cold spot about 16°F cooler at night, as reported Sept. 16, 2026, on NASA Science and in Science Advances.
