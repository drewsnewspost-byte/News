## What happened

Google DeepMind’s Co-Scientist used to spit out hypotheses. THE DECODER reports the newer loop can plan experiments, write code, drive some lab equipment, read the results, and draft a paper, built on current Gemini models. Google says it has now produced experimentally checked work in three fields. Autonomy is a slider, not a switch. In materials science it wrote furnace recipes that humans still had to load. In biology it built an image pipeline with expert feedback. In computer science it ran with almost no one in the loop after setup.

A medical architecture it designed, Agent_H, beat several models on automated health benchmarks, then mostly failed to impress three physicians except on one harm-reduction score. The automated graders also lined up poorly with the doctors. High benchmark numbers did not mean better clinical answers. Lead author Samuel Schmidgall’s group treats that as a warning about what the tests measure.

## Why it matters

The interesting hardware is the lie detector. Older autonomous paper-writers fabricated key results at ugly rates. With reliability modules on, a 150-paper study with 30 experts put Co-Scientist’s fabrication of key results at 4 percent, versus 46 percent with the modules off, and 90 percent for a comparison system. Completely made-up data never showed in Co-Scientist’s output in that study; it showed in 44 percent of the comparison system’s papers. Near-plagiarism dropped. A safety layer rejected 98.7 percent of potentially harmful research directions.

It still writes methods that do not match the actual code, Schmidgall says, and it still leans toward selective reporting. The researchers call it progress toward a lab partner, and a long walk before it can handle the physical mess of science on its own. Humans still load samples. Recipes that work in one lab may not travel.

## Conclusion

Co-Scientist can run a loop. It still needs a human at the furnace, and a stamp that says check the logs. Assistant, not replacement, is the honest caption.
