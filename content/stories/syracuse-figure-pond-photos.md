What happened

On Aug. 10, around 1 p.m., a 25-year-old hiker identified as K.D. was walking near a glacial pond and bog in Onondaga County, New York, when she raised her iPhone 17 and caught two frames of a hunched figure in the water. Gray Television’s KOLD station, filing Aug. 29, 2026, reports that she first read the silhouette as a naked man, then watched it look at her “in such a strange way.” Later, talking to the Bigfoot Field Researchers Organization, she put a harder edge on the memory: “It really looked inhuman.” Those two phone photos are what the cryptid internet is now calling the Syracuse Figure.

BFRO investigator Ben C., described in the KOLD piece as about six feet and athletic, went back to the same shoreline and spent ten to fifteen minutes pushing through mud to recreate her vantage. From that redo he estimates the figure at roughly four feet nine inches—short enough to unsettle the “adult human skinny-dipper” reading and short enough to feed the juvenile-sasquatch camp. Matthew Moneymaker’s orbit is part of the same conversation the station maps: classic BFRO framing, not a courtroom. The photos do not settle taxonomy. They settle a debate that already has a nickname.

Why it matters

Cryptid folklore runs on exactly this kind of ambiguous daylight evidence: a real place, a real clock time, a consumer camera, and a body that refuses to sit still under one label. KOLD’s Aug. 29 package keeps both theories on the table—juvenile sasquatch versus a human skinny-dipper—without turning the bog into a crime scene. That is the entertainment lane this desk stays in. The strange look, the inhuman later quote, the muddy recreation, and the 4'9" estimate are the beats that make the Syracuse Figure travel as pop-history mystery rather than as anything about governments or cabals.

The same Gray/KOLD report also nods to Pennsylvania sightings dated Aug. 18–19 of three-to-five-foot fur figures, another small-stature chapter in the same summer scrapbook. Stack those next to Onondaga County and you get a regional folklore run of short, furred silhouettes—the sort of pattern amateur researchers love and professional zoologists leave alone. Nobody has a type specimen. Everybody has a phone. The strip’s job is to keep the joke and the chill in the same four panels: the pond shot, the look, the mud walk, and the split between a little sasquatch and a wet human who probably regrets the hike timing.
